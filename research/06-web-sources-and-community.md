# 06: Web sources and community voices

Unlike files 01 to 05, this file comes from real web searches. I ran 20 WebSearch queries on 2026-11-27. **Method caveat:** the search tool returns page titles, URLs and short snippets. I did not fetch full pages, so every paraphrase below comes from a snippet. Reddit was hard to reach: most "reddit ..." queries returned itch.io comments, editorial lists, or a Reddit mirror (lr.in.psf.lt and reddit.rtrace.io) instead of threads. No X/Twitter threads turned up at all; a query aimed at Twitter returned only articles. Where a finding is secondhand, I say so.

## 1. Per search topic: what people and developers said

### Horror without enemies (r/gamedesign and others)
- An r/gamedesign thread asked how to make a player paranoid with no enemies. One reply said hearing a ghost's laugh is scarier than seeing her. Another warned that **players quickly notice when nothing can actually hurt them**, and then the tension collapses. (Source: a Reddit mirror.)
- GamesBeat says later Silent Hill games, and Shattered Memories in particular (which has no weapons), traded enemies for atmosphere.
- A Game Developer article suggests dropping the word "enemy", because it implies a fight rather than dread.
- A Unity forum poster argued that a horror game still needs *some* moments of immediate danger the player has to deal with, though not as the main scare.
- **For Unsent:** the warning that players catch on fast is real. Unsent needs at least one moment that *feels* consequential, such as the door room, so players don't conclude early that nothing can happen.

### Underrated horror mechanics
- The results were editorial lists (Den of Geek, GameRant, Dread Central). The mechanics they mention: a sanity meter the player has to watch, lidar scanning in *The Voidness*, proximity voice chat, and sound design in *Stay Out of the House*.

### Walking sims that actually scared people
- Itch.io commenters on *Almost Home Now* (timeberwell) credit its **camera angles and footstep sounds** for unnerving them. Another commenter described "dread so powerful" they didn't want to keep walking.
- Screen Rant's ranking of scary walking sims leads with Layers of Fear.

### Itch.io short narrative horror
- There is no good curated list. The itch.io tag pages (horror + short + narrative, and the half-hour duration filter) are the real discovery tool. Commenters compare some shorts to the film *Skinamarink*. One title found: *Torn* ("Memories will tear you apart"), whose theme overlaps with Unsent's.

### Haunted PS1 Demo Discs
- Outlets most often praise *Effigy* (2020), and *Lorn's Lure*, *EchoStasis* and *Chasing Static* (2021). TechRaptor named *Chasing Static* its pick of the 2021 disc. Wikipedia says one club named the 2020 disc among the best games of 2020.

### Mouthwashing (Wrong Organ)
- There are interviews with producer Kai Moore (PAX West 2024, The Geekly Grind; and EIP Gaming/OpenCritic). A Game*Spark interview from BitSummit 2025 (Japanese, spoiler-heavy) talks to Johanna Kasurinen and Jeffrey Tomec. It says **"responsibility"** is the game's theme and covers the "worst plumbing puzzle". I could only read its opening.
- VGFacts reports, citing a Washington Post interview secondhand, that the story's dilemma came from the team's own troubles on an earlier cancelled project. I have not verified this.
- Nordic Game hosted a session called "Composing Music and SFX for Mouthwashing".

### The Exit 8
- Automaton has an interview with the developer (Kotake Create). He built it in Unreal Engine 5 in about 9 months, kept the core features and cut costly options, and took the looping idea from *Twelve Minutes*.
- One review says its "primary verb is checking". Another critic says it builds creeping anxiety rather than jump scares; even the worst anomaly just fades to black. Because anomalies arrive in random order, **the player manufactures much of the paranoia themselves**.
- Dissent: one reviewer found it interesting "but not one of substance".

### Unpacking (GDC 2022)
- The real talk is **"Unpacking a Narrative Through 1,000 Household Items"** by Wren Brier and Tim Dawson, in GDC 2022's Independent Games Summit. Game Developer's write-up says:
  - Having to place an item makes players think about it carefully.
  - The game never shows what happens between moves, on purpose, so players form their own theories.
  - The order items come out suggests time passing, and some objects outlast earlier owners.

### Before Your Eyes
- Game Developer, GameSpot and Prima interviewed the developers (Bela Messex, Graham Parkes, Oliver Lewin):
  - The game grew out of a student project, *Close Your*, which won awards at IndieCade and the IGF in 2014 and 2015.
  - Messex worried the blink was "too powerful" and could undermine the story. Blinks don't advance the story one-to-one; there is a blink counter.
  - The late closed-eye listening scenes were a deliberate inversion inspired by the endings of *Journey* and *Inside*: after a whole game of trying not to blink, the player is now asked to keep their eyes shut.

### Florence (Ken Wong / Mountains)
- In Wong's words, the core idea was a relationship told "through a 3D jigsaw puzzle". He says they "stopped calling them puzzles"; "interactions" is the better word.
- He describes looking for touch metaphors: how dragging pieces or tapping objects away could stand for an event in a relationship.
- A **playtest lesson**: players focused on solving the puzzle and ignored what the pictures showed, so the team changed the scene to use a clock. Game Informer reports from GDC 2019 that Florence began as a "3D Rubik's cube of human heads".

### Grief mechanics
- The results came from articles, not Reddit. Games that keep coming up: *Spiritfarer* (the spirit tells you when it is ready to go through the Everdoor), *Gris* (a new ability for each stage of grief), *A Mortician's Tale*, *Aka* (just sitting by a pond), *Venba*, *Lost Words*, *Cozy Grove*.
- AV Club ran a piece titled "Grief Should Be Communal in Games".
- NPR ran a piece in January 2026, "how a video game about grief became a breakout hit". The game it covers wasn't visible in the snippet.

### "Game that made me cry"
- Kotaku described a Reddit thread on whether sadness belongs in games. The most-named game in these results is Telltale's *The Walking Dead*.
- **Warning:** a Screen Rant snippet credits *That Dragon, Cancer* with a "blinking mechanic". That mechanic belongs to *Before Your Eyes*, so this is probably an error or a garbled snippet. Don't repeat it.

### Fatal Frame camera
- In GamesRadar, series creator Makoto Shibata says the camera exists to make players look straight at something scary and draw it in as close as possible. This is the strongest primary-source quote I found.
- Critics add three points: the viewfinder narrows your vision, aiming leaves you exposed, and you can't run.
- Siliconera dissents: combat can feel more like a scoring game than a scare.

### Mirror mechanics
- *Wayward Harbor* (free): the monster is visible only in a handheld mirror. A student review called it "unique but not groundbreaking", better as one part of a larger story.
- *A Mirror, Darkly* (Steam app 3602170): you use a mirror to look behind you and spot objects that look different in the reflection.
- An itch.io jam game shows your health as how distorted your reflection is.
- Itch.io feedback: mirror mechanics feel thin unless the game gives the mirror a real use.

### Answering machine and phone horror
- *Home Safety Hotline* (Night Signal Network): you work a mid-90s help line. One reviewer said it starts disturbing and slowly "evolves into cheese".
- *Don't Hang Up*: a 10 to 15 minute Steam short.
- A Steam game that opens with a stranger calling at 3:33 AM who knows your name.
- Itch.io has several "weird voicemail" games.

### Paratopic
- Game Developer's "Road to the IGF" interview says Doc Burford wanted to avoid antagonistic mechanics that could stop the player from continuing. The game began as separate, unrelated vignettes before the team settled on a traditional narrative arc.
- A separate Game Developer article covers Paratopic's unsettling camerawork.
- In an Into The Spine interview (2018), Jessica Harvey says she kept "What Would Doc Do" in mind while building it.

### Dread vs jump scares
- *Darkwood*'s developers chose dread over jump scares (GoNintendo).
- In PC Gamer, Frictional's Thomas Grip says the most important part of a jump scare is the threat of one. Red Barrels' Philippe Morin calls them an "ingredient".
- A 2026 piece (The Technovore) blames streamer clip culture for indie horror's reliance on jump scares.

### That Dragon, Cancer
- In TechCrunch, Ryan Green says the aim was to "mourn in the way we knew how" and invite others to mourn with them.
- Critics agree on the key design point: there is no option or quest that lets you fix Joel. A Waikato paper says the game refuses to make death something you can repeat, avoid or defeat.

### Childhood-home horror
- I found a Steam game (app 3916390) with Unsent's premise: you return to your childhood home and uncover what happened there, in 40 to 60 minutes. The snippet had no title. **Check this listing to avoid looking like a copy.** Visage, Gone Home, Little Nightmares (Vice on "home is where the horror is") and Fran Bow also came up.

## 4. New underrated mechanics and how each fits an Unsent room

1. **A mirror that shows what the room doesn't** (Wayward Harbor, A Mirror, Darkly). Bedroom or hallway: the ghost memory, with an *unpixelated* face for a moment, appears only in the dresser mirror. Use it once, since mirrors get stale fast according to the itch.io feedback.
2. **A distorted reflection as hidden state** (itch.io jam game). Bathroom mirror: how much the reflection warps tracks how many memories you have avoided so far. You never see a meter.
3. **The answering machine as a hub** (Home Safety Hotline, Don't Hang Up). Photo/letter room: tapes of the parent's unsent messages; each room you finish unlocks one. Keep the voices sincere to avoid the "evolves into cheese" problem.
4. **Gaps on purpose** (the Unpacking talk). Suitcase room: never show the moment of leaving. The items you pack are the gap the player fills in themselves.
5. **Stop calling it a puzzle** (Florence). Torn drawing: test whether players actually look at the drawing. If they only solve the shapes, swap in a picture that carries the story by itself (Florence used a clock).
6. **Make players look at the face** (Fatal Frame). Fire room or ending: the player has to hold the camera, or their gaze, on a pixelated face for the pixels to resolve. Looking at it is the cost.
7. **Turn the input around at the end** (Before Your Eyes' closed-eye scenes). Fire room: after a whole game of pressing to interact, the last memory plays only while the player *does nothing*. Releasing all input is the goodbye.
8. **Players create their own paranoia** (Exit 8's random anomalies). Hallway between rooms: shuffle small details on each pass (a coat hook, a photo's angle). They don't mean anything, but players will read meaning into them.
9. **Footsteps and camera angle as the scare** (Almost Home Now). Floorboard foley that answers the player's steps half a beat late.
10. **No fail state** (Paratopic's "no antagonistic mechanics"; That Dragon, Cancer). Door room: the argument can't be won or lost, so the door always opens, but give it real-feeling stakes to counter the "players catch on" problem.
11. **The spirit says when it is ready** (Spiritfarer). Knitting room: the ghost mother sets the pace, and the scene ends when *she* puts down the needles, not when you finish.
