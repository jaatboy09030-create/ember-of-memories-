# 02 — Mechanics as Meaning (Unsent)

> How we did the research: we wrote this from what we already know about well-documented games and talks. **No live web search was run this session.** The URLs below are canonical pages (Wikipedia, GDC Vault, developer sites). Check them before you quote them. Every claim about a game below is widely documented. Treat anything marked *(recalled)* as needing a check.

## 1. Diagnosis: why the current mechanics feel generic

The current mini-games test **skill** (timing, mashing, rotating, sorting), but the story is about **feelings** (fading memory, helplessness, guilt, leaving, regret). When the action under the player's hands doesn't match what the scene means, the mini-game reads as a chore you do to unlock the next cutscene. Three problems run through all six rooms:

1. **Every mechanic is "do it right, get colour back".** Success always feels the same, so the rooms feel interchangeable.
2. **The input never changes over time.** Florence, Brothers and Before Your Eyes all change the same input partway through so that it means something new. We never do.
3. **No mechanic knows about the twist.** The twist ("you left and never came back") should make the player re-read something they did with their own hands. Right now it only changes what they think about cutscenes.

The suspense problem has the same cause. Suspense needs something the player can **lose** or **fail to stop**, and it needs the player to **see it coming**. Our mini-games can't be lost (except the door, and that loss is scripted and obvious).

## 2. Reference games: what they did and why it worked

| Game | Mechanic | Why it works (the lesson for us) |
|---|---|---|
| **Brothers: A Tale of Two Sons** (Starbreeze, 2013) | One stick per brother. After the older brother dies, the younger has to swim, and you press the *dead brother's* input to do it. | The control scheme you learned over the whole game *becomes* the grief. **Lesson: teach a verb early, then take it away or hand it over at the climax.** |
| **Before Your Eyes** (GoodbyeWorld, 2021) | Webcam blinks move time forward. You can't stop blinking, so you skip memories you wanted to keep. | Your own body takes the memories away. **Lesson: an input you can't fully control can stand for memory slipping away.** |
| **Florence** (Mountains, 2018) | Conversation puzzles have fewer pieces as the couple grows close and more as they drift apart. Assembling = getting along. | Difficulty is the relationship. **Lesson: puzzle difficulty itself can be the story's graph.** |
| **Unpacking** (Witch Beam, 2021) | The game won't let you put the ex's diploma on the wall. Where an object is allowed to go tells the story. | No text. The *refusal* is the story. **Lesson: say it with rules about where objects can go, not with dialogue.** |
| **What Remains of Edith Finch – Lewis/cannery** | One stick cuts fish, the other runs a daydream. Over time the daydream takes over the screen. | You physically feel the split attention and the escape. **Lesson: two inputs that compete can stand for a divided mind.** |
| **Papers, Please** (Lucas Pope, 2013) | Boring stamping. Rules get worse. Fail and your family starves. | It's tedious on purpose, and the guilt piles up through small choices. **Lesson: 'boring' works when every choice costs someone something.** |
| **Spiritfarer** (Thunder Lotus, 2020) | You hug each spirit and then row them to the Everdoor yourself. | You perform the goodbye; nobody does it for you. **Lesson: make the player do the letting go.** |
| **That Dragon, Cancer** (Numinous, 2016) | Some scenes have no win state, like the crying-baby scene where nothing you do helps. | Being helpless *is* the point. **Lesson: an input that does nothing, when you can see it does nothing, is devastating, but only if you use it once.** |
| **A Mortician's Tale** (Laundry Bear, 2017) | Slow, gentle, step-by-step body preparation. | Doing it carefully counts as respect. **Lesson: slowness and care can be the whole mechanic.** |
| **Assemble with Care** (ustwo, 2019) | Fixing objects runs alongside repairing a family. | The small physical fix stands in for the emotional repair. |
| **Wilmot's Warehouse** (2019) | You invent your own sorting system and have to remember it. | Memory is the player's own and fallible. |
| **Gris / Journey** | Colour and abilities come back as you work through grief. Journey's chirp is your only voice, and a stranger can share it with you. | Restoring colour is our current mechanic, but Gris ties each colour to a *new verb*. Ours is only decoration. |
| **Passage** (Jason Rohrer, 2007) | The screen is 5 minutes of life. The future is blurred, the past compressed. A partner halves which spaces you can reach. | A tiny game with an enormous statement. Space itself means time. |
| **Kentucky Route Zero** | You choose how characters *feel* rather than what they do. | Player choices express mood rather than change the outcome. |
| **Hellblade: Senua's Sacrifice** | The voices comment on what you do, and the permadeath threat is a bluff. | The game turns its own systems against you. Suspense through untrustworthy rules. |
| **Celeste** | Assist mode. The climb stands for anxiety. Badeline's dash is what finally helps. | Accepting the part of yourself you were fighting becomes a new power. |
| **Who's Lila?** | You move a face's features with the mouse to "act out" expressions. | The **face is the input**. This is very relevant to our pixelated faces. |
| **Lost Words: Beyond the Page** | You walk on the words of a diary and move them to fix the world. | Writing is something you physically do in the level. |
| **Old Man's Journey** | You reshape the land to get through, and memories come back. | The landscape changes to show the memory. |
| **Mouthwashing** (Wrong Organ, 2024) | It retells the same events from another point of view. One scene forces an ugly action on you through a normal "interact" prompt. | The plain interact prompt becomes complicity. **This is the closest model for our twist.** |
| **Silent Hill 2** | Rooms and monsters reflect James's guilt. The ending depends on what you did, not what you chose. | The game reads your behaviour (looking at the letter, healing yourself) and judges you quietly. |
| **Undertale** | It remembers what you did, even across resets. Mercy is a mechanic. | The game remembers and confronts you about your choices. |
| **Obscure / itch.io** *(recalled; check these)*: *Dys4ia* (Anna Anthropy), *Papa Y Yo*, *Gone Home*'s object-reading, *The Graveyard* (Tale of Tales; walking slowly is the whole game), *Rain Rain* / *Loneliness* (Jordan Magnuson, notgames). Magnuson's "notgames" are tiny itch-style pieces where the controls themselves say the thing. | Tiny scope, one strong verb. That fits our team size. |

**Pattern summary.** The strongest examples do one of four things:
- **(a)** Teach a verb, then take it away or hand it over (Brothers, Celeste).
- **(b)** Make difficulty track the feeling (Florence).
- **(c)** Let the rules about objects tell the story (Unpacking, Papers Please).
- **(d)** Have the game remember what you did and show it back to you after a reveal (Undertale, Mouthwashing, Silent Hill 2).

## 3. Cross-game ideas (highest leverage, do these first)

### X1. One recurring verb: **"Hold"** (S–M)
Every memory is resolved by *holding* one button rather than mashing or clicking. You hold the needle, hold the paper together, hold the door, hold an item before packing it, hold the photo, and hold the match.
- The hold timer is shown as the **Ember's glow brightening**.
- After the twist, the final fireplace hold is **long**. The game is asking you to keep the fire going the way the parent did, for years.
- Letting go too early never fails you. The fire just stays smaller, and the ending text reflects that.

This gives the game one physical identity, the way Brothers has its two sticks.

### X2. The Ember is the parent's attention (M)
The twist recast: the Ember is the fire the parent kept going *for you*. Make that something you can feel through mechanics:
- The Ember drifts *toward* objects the parent cared about and lingers there.
- It slowly dims when you ignore its pull or stand still too long.

After the twist the player realises they have been led around by the parent all along. **Brothers-style hand-off:** in the finale the Ember goes out, and the *player* has to carry the light (a match or lantern) from room to room back to the hearth. The parent's role is now the player's verb.

### X3. Faces are the mechanic (M)
The pixelated faces are the game's only unique hook, so turn them into a system:
- Faces get *less* pixelated when the player looks at them steadily (a hold, again).
- Faces snap back to mosaic if the player looks away.
- The **parent's** face never clears until the photo room.
- The **child's own face** (the young ghost) clears early in every room. After the twist the child's face starts *pixelating again*, because the parent has lost the image of the child they never saw grow up.

This is the Who's Lila? lesson: the face carries the meaning.

### X4. The game remembers (S)
Log small player behaviours:
- how long they lingered;
- whether they skipped the parent's lines;
- which item they left behind (room 4);
- whether they read the letter fully.

The final letter and ending lines quote those behaviours back ("You always left before I finished talking."). This costs little (a dictionary in an autoload) and gives Undertale/Silent Hill-style quiet judgement. It also creates **suspense**: once the player notices the game is watching, every action starts to feel loaded.

### X5. Mechanics that change meaning after the twist (M)
On a second pass through, or through the final hallway walk, repeat earlier inputs with the roles reversed:
- The knitting rhythm comes back as the parent's knitting alone, one needle, and it doesn't work.
- The door hold comes back as the parent holding the *front* door open in the snow, waiting.

The same input with a new owner, the way the swimming moment works in Brothers.

### X6. Suspense through loss you can see coming (S)
Add a visible, slow, irreversible drain inside each memory, such as colour bleeding out or snow creeping in through the door. Keep it hard to fail mechanically but real in the narrative: anything you didn't finish is permanently grey in the final house. Suspense = "I can see what I'm going to lose."

## 4. Per-room critique and proposals

Effort guide: **S** ≤ 1 day, **M** 2–4 days, **L** 1+ week for our team in Godot 4.

### Room 1 — Knitting the scarf
**Why it's generic.** Alternating presses is a QTE rhythm game. It tests timing, not memory or love, and nothing says *who* taught you or *who* it's for.

1. **The rhythm you're forgetting (S).** The parent's ghost knits a slow rhythm and you follow it. Each row the parent's hands fade a little more, so the rhythm cues become fainter (visual and audio), and the player has to remember the pattern. Mistakes don't fail you. They make a visible dropped stitch, which stays in the scarf for the rest of the game, worn by the ghost in the final scene. *Meaning: memory fading; flaws kept with love.*
2. **Four hands, two needles (M).** Left input = parent's needle, right input = child's needle (Brothers-style split). At first the parent's needle moves by itself and the child only answers. Gradually the parent's needle stops and the player has to press both. *Meaning: they taught you until you could do it alone, then you left.* After the twist, X5 repeats this with only the parent's needle.
3. **Knit by holding (S, uses X1).** Each stitch is a hold-and-release whose length matches the ghost's breathing. Rushing makes the yarn tight and grey; matching the breathing makes it gold. *Meaning: patience and attunement.*

### Room 2 — Torn drawing scraps
**Why it's generic.** Collecting things plus rotating jigsaw pieces is a standard hidden-object puzzle. It doesn't say *who tore it* or *why*.

1. **The pieces don't fit any more (M).** Some scraps are missing for good. The parent has redrawn them in a shaky adult hand on different paper. The player places these replacement pieces too, and they *almost* fit. *Meaning: the parent filling in a child's world they weren't there for.* This sets up the twist.
2. **Florence difficulty (S).** The early pieces (happy scenes) snap together easily. The pieces near the torn edge (your own anger) resist: they rotate slowly and jitter. *Meaning: difficulty follows the emotion; you tore it.*
3. **Tape, not magic (S).** Fixed pieces are visibly taped and the tape stays on. In the ending the drawing on the fridge shows years of re-taping layers. *Meaning: someone kept repairing this.*

### Room 3 — Holding the study door during the argument
**Why it's generic.** Mashing is the least expressive input there is, and "always lose" is predictable, so it reads as unfair rather than helpless.

1. **Hold, then let go (S, uses X1).** Switch mashing for a steady *hold*. The voices get louder while the hold meter stays fine: the player isn't losing strength. After a while the child's grip (the controller vibration and the hand on screen) *loosens by itself* while you're still holding the button. *Meaning: That Dragon, Cancer style helplessness. You did nothing wrong, and it still opened.*
2. **Cover your ears vs hold the door (M).** Two competing actions, like the Edith Finch cannery. Holding the door muffles nothing. Letting go to cover your ears means you hear less but the door creeps open. The player picks which hurt to accept. *Meaning: a child choosing between not hearing and not seeing.* This also creates suspense.
3. **Listen through the keyhole (S).** No struggle at all. You crouch and the argument is muffled; leaning in makes it clearer but colour drains. Lines from the argument come back in the letter (X4). *Meaning: what you chose to hear defined what you believed.* After the twist one muffled line turns out to be the parent defending you.

### Room 4 — Packing the suitcase as colour drains
**Why it's generic.** Inspect-and-pack is a list-completion task. Four items and four slots means no choice and no loss.

1. **Room for three (S).** Give it four or more meaningful items but only room for **three** (Unpacking's rule: the case physically won't close). The item left behind stays in the house, in the same spot, all through the ending, and the letter mentions it (X4). *Meaning: leaving is choosing what to abandon.* This is the single biggest win for the least cost.
2. **The parent keeps unpacking (M).** While you pack, the ghost parent quietly takes one item out and puts it back on the shelf. Packing turns into a small, quiet tug-of-war with no words. The player can let them keep it. *Meaning: they never wanted you to go.* Suspense: the colour drain speeds up each time you take an item back.
3. **Colour goes with what you pack (S).** Each item drains colour from the *room* when packed: you're literally taking the warmth out of the house. The final colour state of the room = whatever you left behind. *Meaning: guilt you can see.*

### Room 5 — Photo, face reveal, letter
**Why it's generic.** Flip a photo and watch a cutscene. The reveal is passive, and the letter is just text to read.

1. **Clear the face yourself (M, uses X3).** The face doesn't reveal itself; the player holds their gaze on the mosaic face to clear it, block by block. The last blocks need the player to move *closer*, which is uncomfortable. The face is *yours*, older, as the parent imagined it (a twist hint). *Meaning: you have to choose to look.*
2. **Write the letter you never sent (M).** Instead of reading the parent's letter, the player is offered dialogue-fragment choices (Kentucky Route Zero style) to write *their* reply. Each sentence the player picks then visibly burns or doesn't get sent, and the title "Unsent" lands. Then the parent's letter shows: it answers the lines you didn't send.
3. **Turn the photo over and over (S).** Flipping the photo back and forth swaps front and back, and the back gains new handwritten dates each time ("still waiting", the years increasing). *Meaning: time passing while you were gone.* Simple, creepy and suspenseful.

### Room 6 — Relighting the fireplace (ending)
**Why it's generic.** A light-the-fire interaction is a door-unlock with a different texture. It doesn't carry the twist.

1. **Keep it going (S–M, uses X1/X2).** Lighting it is easy. *Keeping it going* means holding to blow on it and feeding logs from what you restored in each room (the scarf, the drawing, the item you left). How big the fire gets depends on what you completed or brought along. *Meaning: you do the parent's job at last.*
2. **The Ember is the fire (S).** The Ember companion has to go into the hearth. The player has to push or hold to let it go (Spiritfarer's goodbye). Afterwards the house is lit, but you walk out alone. *Meaning: letting the parent go.*
3. **Nobody is left to tend it (M, for a darker ending).** The player lights it, the camera holds, the player has to keep feeding it, and the game never ends until the player chooses to stop. When they stop, the text reads something like "I kept it going for you." That's helplessness flipped around, and the cost of leaving is now something the player does.

## 5. Recommended minimum set (short game, small team)

1. **X1 Hold verb** across all rooms, **X4 remembering**, and **Room 4 "room for three"**. All S. Together they give identity, meaning and loss.
2. **Room 3 hold-and-loosen**, **Room 1 fading rhythm** and **Room 6 keep-it-going**. S–M.
3. **X3 faces as mechanic** and **Room 5 clear-the-face**. M. This builds on the one thing that already feels unique.
